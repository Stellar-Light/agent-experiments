The price is nobody's business — and still auditable.

On 2026-08-16, two autonomous agents completed a purchase on Stellar testnet. The buyer paid, the seller verified the money arrived and shipped the goods — and the amount they settled on appears nowhere on-chain. Not obscured. Not off-chain. Encrypted, with a designated auditor who can still read it.

What happened, concretely

VEGA, a seller agent, quoted 40 XLM for a signed data product. NOVA, a buyer agent, paid that invoice through OpenZeppelin's Confidential Token contract on Stellar testnet. Open the payment transaction on an explorer: there is no amount. The chain holds elliptic-curve commitments — points that bind the value without revealing it.

Then the interesting part: VEGA didn't take anyone's word that it was paid. It rebuilt its balance from public chain events and decrypted it with its own key — exactly 40 XLM — checked that against the invoice, and only then delivered the goods, signed. NOVA verified the signature. Five transactions, ledgers 4,175,209→4,175,214, about twenty seconds end to end, with each zero-knowledge proof generated in under two seconds on a laptop.

Why agents need this

Agent-to-agent commerce has a pricing problem: on a transparent chain, every negotiated price is public strategy. If your procurement agent pays 40 for what its rival paid 60 for, both numbers are on the record forever — every discount you negotiate, every margin you accept, readable by every competitor's agent in real time. Businesses do not run their purchasing in public, and neither will their agents.

But the fix cannot be full anonymity. Money that regulators and auditors cannot examine is money institutions cannot touch. The shape that works is the one this experiment demonstrates: amounts confidential to the world, decryptable by the parties — and by an auditor whose access is enforced by the protocol itself. The transfer proof literally refuses to verify unless it carries ciphertexts the registered auditor can decrypt. Compliance visibility isn't a promise here; it's a precondition of the money moving.

The demo doesn't replay a recording of this claim — its second mode runs the real client in your browser: it derives the agents' keys from the published testnet session, fetches the chain's events, decrypts both balances in front of you, and checks them against the chain's own commitments. About one second, no server.

What this stands on, and what we added

The cryptography is not ours, and the credits matter:

  • OpenZeppelin Confidential Tokens — the contracts: confidential balances, the UltraHonk verifier, and the auditor registry, deployed on Stellar testnet.
  • stellar-confidential-token-sdk by aguilar1x — the conformant TypeScript client this experiment drives (v0.1.9): key derivation, proof generation, state replay, and the verify-against-chain trust boundary. Its own site is the deep reference for the primitive.

What this experiment adds is the layer above the payment: a commerce protocol between autonomous agents — quote → confidential payment → the seller verifying receipt by decryption instead of by webhook or human confirmation → cryptographically signed delivery → signature verification by the buyer. Payment rails have demos; this is the primitive doing a job: two machines completing a deal with a private price and a public proof. To our knowledge, the first agent-to-agent commerce settled confidentially on Stellar.

The honest limits

  • Testnet, throughout. The client SDK is v0.1.x and unaudited; its own README says do not hold value with it. Nothing here implies mainnet readiness.
  • Confidential ≠ anonymous. The amount is hidden; the participating addresses and the fact-of-payment are public. This is confidential amounts with auditability — not a mixer, and deliberately so.
  • Deposits are public by design. Real XLM entering the contract is visible; privacy begins once value is inside, where balances live as commitments.
  • The published session keys are deliberate — testnet accounts holding nothing, published so the demo can run the real client in your browser (the SDK demo's own precedent).
